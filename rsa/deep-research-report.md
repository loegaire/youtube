## Key Generation
- **00:00–00:15** – *Narration:* RSA is a public-key encryption scheme. It generates a public key pair (n, e) and a private key d so that encrypting with (n,e) can only be undone by d. *Animation:* Two glowing square blocks labeled **p** and **q** slide in and multiply together into a larger square labeled **n**. A smaller square **e** attaches to **n** (forming the public key (n,e) in bright color), while a separate lock-and-key icon appears next to another square **d**, highlighting the private key.  

- **00:15–00:30** – *Narration:* The keys come from two large primes p and q. Multiplying them yields *n = p·q*. We then compute φ(n) = (p–1)(q–1). A public exponent *e* is chosen with 1<e<φ(n) and gcd(e,φ(n))=1, then *d* is found as the modular inverse of *e* mod φ(n) (so that *e·d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n))*). *Animation:* The prime blocks **p** and **q** merge to form **n**. Then copies of p and q shrink by one to show **p–1** and **q–1**, which multiply to illustrate φ(n). A new square **e** drops in and bounces around until it “fits” (symbolizing gcd=1). Finally, an animated Euclidean chart or arcs connect **e** and φ(n) to produce **d**, illustrating the modular inverse calculation (for example, an arc labeled “e·d ≡ 1 mod φ(n)” appears).

## Encryption and Decryption
- **00:30–00:45** – *Narration:* To encrypt a message **M**, it is first converted into an integer (e.g. via padding). The ciphertext is computed as *C ≡ M^e (mod n)* using the public key. *Animation:* A square labeled **M** is shown. Then a big red “mod n” filter (a translucent grid or gate) appears, trimming the stack: only the remainder blocks remain, forming **C = M^e mod n**. The final block **C** glows as the ciphertext.

- **00:45–01:00** – *Narration:* The recipient uses the private key *d* to recover **M** by computing *M ≡ C^d (mod n)*. Because *e·d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n))* this restores the original message. *Animation:* The square **C** is raised to power *d*: it again multiplies into a tall stack of **C** blocks. The same “mod n” filter appears, cutting it down. The remaining block spells out **M** (the original message number), which then transforms back into readable text or a document icon, demonstrating decryption.

## Mathematical Foundations (Euler’s Theorem)
- **01:00–01:15** – *Narration:* This works by Euler’s theorem. In a prime modulus *p*, any number *a* coprime to *p* satisfies *a^{p−1} ≡ 1 (mod p)*. In the RSA setting, with *n=pq*, any *M* coprime to *n* satisfies *M^{φ(n)} ≡ 1 (mod n)*. Since *d·e ≡ 1 (mod φ(n))*, we can write *M^{e·d} = M^{1 + k·φ(n)} ≡ M (mod n)*, recovering the message. *Animation:* Display a circular loop of numbers (e.g. 1 through 6 around a circle for mod 7). A marker at 5 moves through powers: **5 → 4 → 6 → 2 → 3 → 1**, illustrating a complete cycle of length 6 and returning to 1 (since 5^6 ≡ 1 mod 7). Text overlay shows “5^6 ≡ 1 (mod 7)”. Then connect to RSA: arrows show that raising **M** first by *e* then by *d* (spanning φ(n) steps in between) brings it back to **M** mod n.

- **01:15–01:30** – *Narration:* Because *n = p·q* is composite, only numbers coprime to *n* (there are φ(n) of them) form the multiplicative group mod n. We work modulo φ(n) for exponents so every element has an inverse. In short, exponentiation “wraps around” at φ(n) rather than n, ensuring *e* and *d* exist. *Animation:* Show two overlapping circles labeled **p−1** and **q−1**, highlighting their product φ(n). A number line from 1 to *n* appears with non-coprime numbers (multiples of p or q) grayed out and coprime numbers highlighted. Arrows depict exponents *e* and *d* as steps that loop mod φ(n) (e.g., a slider moving from 0 to φ(n)).

## Computational Performance and Key Generation Cost
- **01:30–01:45** – *Narration:* Finding large primes for p and q is computationally expensive. Probabilistic primality tests rapidly eliminate non-primes. By the prime number theorem, around a number *N* there is roughly 1 prime per ln(N) candidates. Thus, on average we must test *O(ln(p))* numbers to find a prime of size *p*. In practice this means trying many candidates until two big primes emerge. *Animation:* A rapid scroll of random large numbers (e.g. 100-digit block) is shown, each being tested (perhaps struck out with an “X” if composite). One by one they pass or fail a primality test, until two numbers finally glow “prime.” Visual counters or a progress bar can emphasize many trials occur before success.

- **01:45–02:00** – *Narration:* Encrypting or decrypting involves modular exponentiation, which is optimized. We use the property *(a·b mod n) = (a mod n)·(b mod n) mod n* to reduce intermediate values, and *exponentiation by squaring* (square-and-multiply) to cut the number of multiplications from *e* down to about *log(e)*. *Animation:* Contrast naive vs fast exponentiation: for example, to compute *a^8*, show eight multiplications in a row (slow). Then animate the fast way: square **a** to get **a^2**, square that to **a^4**, then square to **a^8** (only 3 multiplications). Each intermediate result flows through a small “mod n” filter, keeping block sizes bounded. A timer or step count ticks much faster for the square-and-multiply path, illustrating the speedup.

## CRT Decryption Optimization
- **02:00–02:20** – *Narration:* Decryption can be sped up by splitting the calculation via the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT). Instead of computing *C^d mod n* in one step, compute *m₁ = C^d mod p* and *m₂ = C^d mod q* separately. Since p and q are half the bit-size of n, these exponentiations are much faster (roughly a 4× speedup overall). *Animation:* The ciphertext **C** flows into two parallel branches. In one branch, **C** enters a “mod p” module and undergoes exponentiation (show block multiplication) producing **C_p**; in the other branch, it enters a “mod q” module producing **C_q**. The blocks **C_p** and **C_q** emerge from each branch. Animated arrows or a pipeline emphasize that each branch handles smaller numbers.

- **02:20–02:40** – *Narration:* Finally we recombine the two results using Garner’s formula. Since we know *M ≡ C_p (mod p)* and *M ≡ C_q (mod q)*, the unique solution mod *n* is  
\[
M = C_q + q \cdot \bigl((C_p - C_q)\cdot q^{-1} \bmod p\bigr).
\] 
Here *q^{-1}* mod *p* is precomputed. *Animation:* Represent the calculation: draw **C_p** and **C_q** blocks feeding into a formula box. Inside, animate taking **(C_p−C_q)** (subtract block), multiplying by the precomputed block “*q<sup>-1</sup> mod p*”, then taking mod *p*. The result multiplies a **q** block and adds **C_q** to produce the final **M** block. The steps can light up in sequence (difference → multiply by inverse → multiply by q → add C_q) culminating in **M**.

- **02:40–02:55** – *Narration:* We can speed up each branch further by reducing the exponent: compute *d_p = d mod (p−1)* and *d_q = d mod (q−1)* (Fermat’s little theorem). Then calculate *m₁ = C^{d_p} mod p* and *m₂ = C^{d_q} mod q*. Since *d_p,d_q < p,q*, these exponentiations are much quicker. *Animation:* In each branch’s exponentiation block, the exponent label shrinks from “d” to “d_p” (or “d_q”). Display the exponents dropping to size p−1 or q−1 (like a bar getting shorter). Show a faster loop of squaring/multiplying within each mod p or mod q environment, and highlight that the work is greatly reduced.

## Security Considerations
- **02:55–03:10** – *Narration:* RSA’s security comes from the hardness of factoring large n. Keys too small (e.g. 512-bit) can be broken; current guidance is to use 2048-bit or larger keys. Increasing *n*’s size dramatically increases factorization cost. As [9†L830-L834] notes, minimum recommendations have moved to at least 2048 bits to remain secure. *Animation:* Show a bar or scale of bit lengths: 512-bit is red and shattered, 1024-bit is yellow and cracked, 2048-bit and above are green and locked. A cartoon “crusher” (representing brute-force) fails to break the large blue block **n**, highlighting its strength.

- **03:10–03:30** – *Narration:* Attackers also try smarter math: factoring *n* or finding φ(n) or *d*. To resist these, primes must be chosen carefully. For example, avoid *p* and *q* that are too close (which makes Fermat’s factorization easy) and ensure *p−1* and *q−1* each have a large prime factor (thwarting Pollard’s p−1 method). Also ensure *p* and *q* are independent (so they don’t share factors in *p−1,q−1*). *Animation:* Show the modulus **n** being “pulled apart” by factorization. Display a cartoon saw splitting **n** into **p** and **q**. Then show warnings: “p–q small → Fermat attack” with a failed saw attempt, and “p–1 has small factors → Pollard attack” with a broken chain. Finally, show two robust, irregular “strong” primes being chosen instead, resisting the saw.

- **03:30–03:45** – *Narration:* Side-channel attacks like timing attacks can leak information about *d*. For instance, if decryption takes slightly longer on certain inputs, an attacker could infer bits of *d*. Countermeasures include using constant-time algorithms and blinding techniques (adding random delays or multiplying by random factors) so that every decryption takes the same time. *Animation:* Show a clock face and a decryption chip icon. A graph illustrates “time vs input” with spikes revealing data. Then depict a guard adding random “jitter” or a padlock icon, making the time line flat. This indicates that the decryption operation now always takes equal time regardless of input.

- **03:45–04:00** – *Narration:* RSA is malleable under multiplication. A chosen-ciphertext attack could exploit this: for example, an attacker who wants to decrypt **C = M^e** might submit **X = 2^e·C (mod n)** for decryption. The result is *X^d ≡ 2·M (mod n)*; dividing by 2 yields *M*. In practice, secure protocols prevent this by using padding (e.g. OAEP) and refusing to decrypt raw ciphertexts. *Animation:* Illustrate an attacker node taking **C**, multiplying it by **2^e** (shown as attaching a “2” factor), and sending **X** to the decrypter. Show the decrypter returning **D(X) = 2·M**. Then the attacker divides by 2 to get **M**. Finally, overlay a big “PAD” block or shield on **M** to represent secure padding, blocking any attacker from simply querying raw ciphertext decryption.
